# PickTonight Discovery Interview Guide

- **Status:** Working draft
- **Research round:** Week 1 discovery
- **Owner:** ZK Zhao
- **Related issue:** [#2 — Prepare the discovery interview guide](https://github.com/zkzzk34/pick-tonight/issues/2)
- **Target:** At least three interviews, aiming for five
- **Session length:** Approximately 25 to 30 minutes
- **Recording:** No recording by default; private notes only

## Purpose

Learn how adult streaming viewers currently choose movies and television shows, especially when the decision feels difficult.

The interview should investigate actual behavior before evaluating or presenting PickTonight. It should not ask participants to approve the product idea.

## Research questions

1. What triggers a difficult viewing decision?
2. What steps do people take from deciding to watch something to choosing, abandoning, or defaulting to a familiar title?
3. Which criteria and constraints influence the decision?
4. Where do people seek ideas, and what does each source contribute?
5. How do people evaluate fresh titles with limited ratings or reviews?
6. When and why do trailers influence the decision?
7. How do companions affect the process?
8. What creates confidence, friction, overload, or abandonment?
9. Which behaviors recur across participants, and which appear to be exceptions?

## Participant criteria

Recruit adults who:

- use at least one legal streaming service;
- have chosen a movie or television show within the last month;
- sometimes choose alone, with a partner, with friends, or with family;
- are willing to discuss a recent real viewing decision.

Seek variation in:

- viewing frequency;
- usual viewing companions;
- time available for watching;
- sources used to find ideas;
- preference for new versus familiar content;
- content languages and countries of interest.

Do not require participants to use TikTok, ChatGPT, or any other named tool.

The founder's wife may participate because her experience inspired the project, but her behavior must not be treated as representative. Recruit outside the household as well.

## Privacy and note handling

Before each session:

- assign a participant code such as `P01`;
- copy the blank notes template into `research-private/week-1/`;
- keep names and contact details outside the research notes;
- explain that participation is voluntary;
- ask permission before taking notes;
- do not record audio, video, or screens unless separate explicit permission is obtained;
- ask separately whether anonymous paraphrases or short de-identified quotations may be used.

Raw notes must remain outside Git and the public repository.

Participants may request deletion of their raw notes through the private recruitment channel until their information has been de-identified and incorporated into the synthesis. Explain that after de-identification and aggregation, it may no longer be possible to isolate and remove an individual contribution.

Remove accidental identifying or unnecessary sensitive information from notes as soon as possible. Delete raw notes no later than 90 days after the final discovery interview, or earlier after the anonymized synthesis is complete.

Anonymized themes and aggregate findings may remain in the public project documentation.

## Interviewer principles

- Ask about a specific recent event before asking about general habits.
- Use open and neutral questions.
- Ask one question at a time.
- Allow silence so the participant can remember details.
- Follow the participant's language rather than introducing product terminology.
- Ask what happened before asking why.
- Separate reported behavior from your interpretation.
- Ask for examples when answers become hypothetical or general.
- Do not correct, teach, sell, or defend the product.
- Do not show a prototype or explain PickTonight until the behavioral interview is complete.
- Treat contradictions and unusual behavior as evidence rather than mistakes.

Useful follow-ups include:

- “Can you walk me through that?”
- “What happened next?”
- “What made that important at the time?”
- “Can you give me a recent example?”
- “What did you do after that?”
- “How did you decide?”
- “What, if anything, changed your mind?”
- “You mentioned that earlier. How did it affect this decision?”

## Introduction and permission script

> Thank you for speaking with me. I am working on a non-commercial portfolio project about how people choose movies and television shows.
>
> I want to learn about your current experience. I am not testing you, and there are no correct answers. I will not show or ask you to evaluate my product idea during the interview.
>
> This should take about 25 to 30 minutes. Participation is voluntary. You may skip any question or stop at any time.
>
> I would like to take written notes, but I will not record the session. The raw notes will be stored privately under a participant code, and any public findings will be anonymized. I will not place your name or contact information in the research notes. Raw notes will be deleted no later than 90 days after the final discovery interview.
>
> If you want me to delete your raw notes before I anonymize and combine the findings, you may contact me through the same private method used to arrange this interview. After the findings have been de-identified and combined, I may no longer be able to isolate your individual contribution.
>
> Are you comfortable participating under those conditions, and do I have your permission to take notes?

Wait for an affirmative response before beginning.

Then ask:

> May I also use a short anonymous paraphrase or de-identified quotation in the project review? Saying no will not affect your participation.

Record both answers in the private notes.

## Interview guide

The times below are flexible ceilings, not requirements to ask every question. Prioritize the participant's recent decision and the most relevant follow-ups; skip prompts that were already answered or do not apply.

### 1. Warm-up and viewing context — 2 minutes

1. Tell me a little about when you usually watch movies or television.
2. Who, if anyone, do you usually watch with?
3. How often do you personally help decide what to watch?

Keep this section brief. Do not collect unrelated demographic information.

### 2. Most recent difficult decision — 7 minutes

> Think about the most recent time you wanted to watch something but had difficulty choosing. Please walk me through what happened from the moment you decided to watch something.

Follow the participant's sequence before introducing prompts.

Possible follow-ups:

- When did this happen?
- Who was involved?
- How much time did you have?
- What was the first thing you did?
- What options did you consider?
- What did you do next?
- Approximately how long did the decision take?
- What did you eventually choose?
- What made that option acceptable?
- How confident did you feel?
- Did you start watching it?
- If you did not choose anything, what happened instead?

Do not infer that watch intent means the title was actually watched.

### 3. Decision criteria and constraints — 3 minutes

1. What mattered most during that decision?
2. Which factors became requirements, and which were only preferences?
3. Was anything immediately ruled out?
4. Did any constraint change while you were deciding?

Only after spontaneous answers, probe relevant areas such as:

- mood;
- available time;
- movie versus television;
- genre;
- companions;
- content language or country;
- streaming availability;
- familiarity or freshness;
- rating or review information;
- content concerns.

Do not read the full list unless the participant needs help recalling the situation.

### 4. Sources and handoffs — 5 minutes

1. Where did you look or whom did you ask for ideas?
2. In what order did you use those sources?
3. What were you hoping to get from each one?
4. What did one source provide that another did not?
5. Did you have to move between tools to verify or evaluate a suggestion?
6. What, if anything, made information from a source feel trustworthy or untrustworthy?
7. Where did the process become repetitive or frustrating?

Let the participant name sources first.

Only after spontaneous recall is exhausted, say:

> People sometimes use streaming home screens, search engines, social media, friends, review sites, trailers, or conversational assistants. Which, if any, have you used in a recent real decision?

For each relevant source, ask for a specific example. Do not assume TikTok, ChatGPT, or another named service is important to everyone.

### 5. New titles and limited information — 2 minutes

1. Tell me about a recent time you considered a new movie or show that had little rating or review information.
2. How did you decide whether it was worth considering?
3. What signals did you use instead?
4. How, if at all, did the limited information affect what you did next?
5. What did you do to reduce uncertainty?

Do not describe low vote counts as evidence that a title is poor.

### 6. Trailers and deeper evaluation — 2 minutes

1. Tell me about the most recent time you watched a trailer before deciding.
2. At what point in the process did you watch it?
3. What were you trying to learn?
4. What made you continue considering or reject the title?
5. When do you skip trailers?

### 7. Companions and negotiation — 2 minutes

If the participant sometimes watches with others:

1. Tell me about the most recent time more than one person was involved in choosing.
2. How were suggestions introduced?
3. How did you resolve disagreements?
4. Whose constraints mattered most?
5. Did the group abandon any otherwise suitable options?

### 8. Abandonment and overload — 2 minutes

1. Tell me about the most recent time you stopped searching, chose a familiar fallback, or watched nothing.
2. What caused that outcome?
3. How many options were you seriously comparing?
4. What makes a set of options feel useful rather than overwhelming?

Do not mention PickTonight's three-result design.

### 9. Wrap-up — 2 minutes

1. What, if anything, is difficult about choosing something to watch today?
2. What works well in your current process?
3. If you could change one part of your current process, what would you change, if anything?
4. Is there anything important about how you choose that I did not ask?

Thank the participant and remind them that their public contribution will be anonymized.

If they ask about PickTonight, explain it only after the interview is complete. Treat any reaction to that explanation as informal feedback, not discovery evidence.

## Questions to avoid

Do not ask:

- “Would you use an app that gives exactly three recommendations?”
- “Do you like the PickTonight idea?”
- “Would conversational input make choosing easier?”
- “Would TikTok integration be useful?”
- “Do ratings make new content hard to choose?”
- “Do you suffer from decision fatigue?”
- “What features should I build?”
- “Wouldn't verified recommendations be better?”

These questions reveal the proposed solution, invite politeness, or ask participants to predict hypothetical behavior.

## After each session

1. Complete the notes while the session is fresh.
2. Remove names, contact details, usernames, precise locations, and unrelated sensitive details.
3. Separate direct observations and participant statements from interpretation.
4. Record contradictions, surprises, and evidence that challenges the product assumptions.
5. Add tentative theme tags without treating a single interview as a general finding.
6. Store the notes only in `research-private/week-1/`.
7. Update the recruitment tracker separately from research notes.
8. Do not publish raw notes.
9. After the round, synthesize recurring patterns, counts, contradictions, and limitations into an anonymized repository document.
